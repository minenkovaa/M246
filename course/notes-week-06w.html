    Section Week 6: Multiple integrals; vector calculus

    Subsection Double Integrals and Signed Volume

    The definite integral of a function of one variable measures signed area. For functions of two variables, a double integral measures signed volume.
    🔗

    Definition 91.

    For a function \(f(x,y)\) defined on a region \(R\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA \end{equation*}
    represents the accumulated value of \(f\) over the region.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Iterated Integrals

    Most double integrals you will encounter are evaluated as repeated one-variable integrals.
    🔗

    Theorem 92.

    \begin{equation*} \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \int_a^b\int_{c(x)}^{d(x)} f(x,y)\,dy\,dx. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 93.

    Evaluate
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1\int_0^2 (x+y)\,dy\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1\int_0^2 (x+y)\,dy\,dx = \int_0^1 \left[xy + \frac{y^2}{2}\right]_0^2 dx = \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = \int_0^1 (2x + 2) dx = \left[x^2 + 2x\right]_0^1 = 3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Changing the Order of Integration

    Sometimes reversing the order of integration greatly simplifies a problem.
    🔗

    Activity 4.

    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1 \int_x^1 f(x,y)\,dy\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    The region is a triangle with vertices at (0,0), (1,1), and (0,1). The integral can be rewritten as
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1 \int_x^1 f(x,y)\,dy\,dx=\int_0^1 \int_0^y f(x,y)\,dx\,dy. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Applications

    Definition 94.

    \begin{equation*} f_{avg} = \frac{1}{A(R)} \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 95.

    Suppose \(f(x,y)\) represents temperature distribution over a metal plate. Interpret
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{A(R)} \iint_Rf\,dA. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Coordinates Type

    In this section we will discuss different coordinate systems that are useful for evaluating integrals in multivariable calculus.
    🔗

    Subsubsection Polar Coordinates

    Circular regions are often easier to describe using polar coordinates.
    🔗
    Definition 96.
    \begin{equation*} x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Theorem 97.
    \begin{equation*} dA=r\,dr\,d\theta. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Example 98.
    Evaluate
    \begin{equation*} \iint_R (x^2+y^2)\,dA \end{equation*}
    where \(R\) is the unit disk.
    🔗
    Solution.
    In polar coordinates, \(x = r\cos\theta\) and \(y = r\sin\theta\text{,}\) so \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\text{.}\) The integral becomes
    \begin{equation*} \iint_R (x^2+y^2)\,dA = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^1 r^2 \cdot r\,dr\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^1 r^3\,dr\,d\theta. \end{equation*}
    Evaluating the inner integral,
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1 r^3\,dr = \left[\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^1 = \frac{1}{4}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{1}{4}\,d\theta = \frac{1}{4} \cdot 2\pi = \frac{\pi}{2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection Cylindrical Coordinates

    Cylindrical coordinates are useful for describing surfaces of revolution and other 3D shapes with circular symmetry.
    🔗
    Definition 99.
    Cylindrical coordinates are defined by
    \begin{equation*} x = r\cos\theta, \quad y = r\sin\theta, \quad z = z. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Theorem 100.
    The volume element in cylindrical coordinates is given by
    \begin{equation*} dV = r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection Spherical Coordinates

    Spherical coordinates are useful for describing spheres and other 3D shapes with radial symmetry.
    🔗
    Definition 101.
    Spherical coordinates are defined by
    \begin{equation*} x = \rho\sin\phi\cos\theta, \quad y = \rho\sin\phi\sin\theta, \quad z = \rho\cos\phi, \end{equation*}
    where \(\rho\) is the radial distance, \(\phi\) is the polar angle, and \(\theta\) is the azimuthal angle.
    🔗
    🔗
    Theorem 102.
    The volume element in spherical coordinates is given by
    \begin{equation*} dV = \rho^2 \sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Vector Fields

    A vector field assigns a vector to every point in a region.
    🔗

    Definition 103.

    \begin{equation*} \mathbf F(x,y) = \langle P(x,y),Q(x,y)\rangle. \end{equation*}
    is a vector-function of two variables with two components.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 104.

    Interpret and graph the vector field
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf F(x,y)=\langle -y,x\rangle. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    The vector field \(\mathbf F(x,y)=\langle -y,x\rangle\) represents a rotational flow around the origin. At any point \((x,y)\text{,}\) the vector points in the direction of the tangent to the circle centered at the origin passing through that point.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection The Gradient

    The gradient links scalar functions and vector fields.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \nabla f = \left\langle f_x, f_y \right\rangle. \end{equation*}

    Theorem 105.

    The gradient points in the direction of greatest increase.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 106.

    Find the gradient of
    \begin{equation*} f(x,y)=x^2+y^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    The gradient is computed as follows:
    \begin{equation*} \nabla f = \left\langle \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \right\rangle = \left\langle 2x, 2y \right\rangle. \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the gradient vector at any point \((x,y)\) is \(\langle 2x, 2y \rangle\text{,}\) which points radially outward from the origin, indicating the direction of greatest increase of the function \(f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Divergence

    Divergence measures expansion or compression of a vector field.
    🔗

    Definition 107.

    Divergence is defined as
    \begin{equation*} Div(\mathbf F) = \nabla\cdot \mathbf F = \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 108.

    Compute the divergence of
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf F = \langle x,y\rangle. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    The divergence is computed as follows:
    \begin{equation*} Div(\mathbf F) = \nabla\cdot \mathbf F = \frac{\partial x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial y}{\partial y} = 1 + 1 = 2. \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the divergence of the vector field \(\mathbf F = \langle x, y \rangle\) is constant and equal to 2, indicating that the field is uniformly expanding at every point in the plane.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Curl

    Curl measures local rotation.
    🔗

    Definition 109.

    For a vector field \(\mathbf F = \langle P(x,y), Q(x,y), R(x,y) \rangle\text{,}\) the curl is defined as
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{curl}(\mathbf F)=\begin{vmatrix} \mathbf i & \mathbf j & \mathbf k \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ P & Q & R \end{vmatrix} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = \left\langle \frac{\partial R}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial Q}{\partial z}, \frac{\partial P}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial R}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \right\rangle. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 110.

    Find the curl of
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf F = \langle -y,x,x+yz\rangle. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    The curl is computed as follows:
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{curl}(\mathbf F) = \nabla \times \mathbf F = \left\langle \frac{\partial (x+yz)}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial x}{\partial z}, \frac{\partial (-y)}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial (x+yz)}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial x}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial (-y)}{\partial x} \right\rangle = \left\langle z - 0, 0 - 1, 0 - 0 \right\rangle = \langle z, -1, 0 \rangle. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Line Integrals

    A line integral adds values along a curve.
    🔗

    Definition 111.

    \begin{equation*} \int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Physical interpretation: work done by a force field.
    🔗

    Example 112.

    Compute the work done by
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf F=\langle 1,2\rangle \end{equation*}
    along a straight path.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The work done by the constant force field \(\mathbf F = \langle 1, 2 \rangle\) along a straight path from point \(A\) to point \(B\) can be computed using the line integral:
    \begin{equation*} W = \int_C \mathbf F \cdot d\mathbf r = \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf d = \langle 1, 2 \rangle \cdot \langle x_B - x_A, y_B - y_A \rangle = 1(x_B - x_A) + 2(y_B - y_A). \end{equation*}
    This formula gives the total work done by the force field along the straight path from \(A\) to \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Conservative Vector Fields

    Definition 113.

    A vector field is conservative if
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf F=\nabla f \end{equation*}
    for some potential function \(f\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 114.

    For a conservative field,
    \begin{equation*} \int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = f(B)-f(A). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 5.

    Find a potential function for
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf F=\langle 2x,2y\rangle. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Integral Theorems in Multivariable Calculus

    Subsubsection Overview

    Integral theorems connect local derivative information (gradient, curl, divergence) with global quantities such as line, surface, and volume integrals. They provide powerful tools for converting difficult integrals into easier ones.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection The Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals

    If \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla f\) is a gradient field and \(C\) is a curve from \(A\) to \(B\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = f(B)-f(A). \end{equation*}
    The line integral depends only on the endpoints, not on the path.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection Green’s Theorem

    For a positively oriented simple closed curve \(C\) enclosing a region \(R\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \oint_C (P\,dx + Q\,dy) = \iint_R \left( \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \right)\,dA. \end{equation*}
    Green’s Theorem converts a boundary line integral into a double integral over the enclosed region.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection Stokes’ Theorem

    Let \(S\) be an oriented surface with boundary \(\partial S\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \oint_{\partial S} \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F}) \cdot \mathbf{n}\,dS. \end{equation*}
    Stokes’ Theorem generalizes Green’s Theorem to surfaces in three dimensions.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection Divergence Theorem

    If \(E\) is a solid region with boundary surface \(\partial E\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} \iint_{\partial E} \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \iiint_E (\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F})\,dV. \end{equation*}
    The total outward flux through a closed surface equals the accumulated divergence inside the volume.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection Summary

    • Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals: gradient ↔ line integral.
      🔗
    • Green’s Theorem: boundary curve ↔ planar region.
      🔗
    • Stokes’ Theorem: boundary curve ↔ surface.
      🔗
    • Divergence Theorem: closed surface ↔ volume.
      🔗
    \begin{equation*} Gradient → Line Integral → Green/Stokes → Divergence \end{equation*}
