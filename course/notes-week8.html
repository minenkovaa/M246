    Subsection Systems of Differential Equations

    Many applications involve several dependent variables that interact simultaneously. Such models naturally lead to systems of differential equations.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}'=A\mathbf{x} \end{equation*}

    Subsubsection Solving Systems Using Eigenvalues

    If \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\) with eigenvector \(\mathbf{v}\text{,}\) then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}(t)=e^{\lambda t}\mathbf{v} \end{equation*}
    is a solution of the system.
    🔗
    Example 38. Worked Example.
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}'= \begin{bmatrix}3 & 1\\1 & 3\end{bmatrix} \mathbf{x} \end{equation*}
    The characteristic equation gives eigenvalues
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_1=4, \qquad \lambda_2=2. \end{equation*}
    Corresponding eigenvectors are
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}_1= \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{v}_2= \begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The general solution is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}(t)= c_1e^{4t}\mathbf{v}_1+ c_2e^{2t}\mathbf{v}_2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Remark 134. Notation.
    • \(\mathbf{x}(t)\) denotes the solution vector.
      🔗
    • \(\lambda\) denotes an eigenvalue of the coefficient matrix \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • \(\mathbf{v}\) denotes an eigenvector corresponding to \(\lambda\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • \(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2\) are eigenvectors associated with distinct eigenvalues \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • \(c_1,c_2\) are arbitrary constants determined by the initial conditions.
      🔗
    • \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) denote the real and imaginary parts of a complex eigenvalue \(\lambda=\alpha\pm i\beta\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • \(\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{p}+i\mathbf{q}\) denotes a complex eigenvector, where \(\mathbf{p}\) and \(\mathbf{q}\) are real vectors.
      🔗
    • For complex eigenvalues, the real-valued solution is obtained from the vectors \(\mathbf{p}\) and \(\mathbf{q}\text{:}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Example 39. Complex Eigenvalues.
    Solve the system
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2\\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{x}. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    Compute the characteristic polynomial:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \det(A-\lambda I) = \begin{vmatrix} 1-\lambda & -2\\ 2 & 1-\lambda \end{vmatrix} =0. \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} (1-\lambda)^2+4=0. \end{equation*}
    The eigenvalues are
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lambda = 1\pm 2i. \end{equation*}
    Choose the eigenvalue \(\lambda=1+2i\text{.}\) Solving \((A-\lambda I)\mathbf{v}=0\) gives the eigenvector
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ -i \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Write the eigenvector as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{p} + i\mathbf{q}, \end{equation*}
    where
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{p} = \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{q} = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ -1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Using the complex solution formula,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}(t) = e^{t} \left[ c_1 \left( \mathbf{p}\cos(2t) - \mathbf{q}\sin(2t) \right) + c_2 \left( \mathbf{p}\sin(2t) + \mathbf{q}\cos(2t) \right) \right]. \end{equation*}
    Substituting \(\mathbf{p}\) and \(\mathbf{q}\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}(t) = c_1e^t \begin{bmatrix} \cos(2t)\\ \sin(2t) \end{bmatrix} + c_2e^t \begin{bmatrix} \sin(2t)\\ -\cos(2t) \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Remark 136. Geometric Interpretation.
    The eigenvalues \(1\pm2i\) have positive real part \(\alpha=1\text{.}\) The factor \(e^t\) causes solutions to grow, while the \(\sin(2t)\) and \(\cos(2t)\) terms produce oscillation. Therefore trajectories spiral away from the origin, and the equilibrium is an unstable spiral.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Laplace Transforms

    Definition 43. Laplace Transform.

    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}\{f(t)\}=F(s)=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-st}f(t)\,dt \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 40. Solving an IVP.

    \begin{equation*} y'+2y=0,\qquad y(0)=3 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} sY-3+2Y=0 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} Y=\frac{3}{s+2} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} y(t)=3e^{-2t} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Subsubsection Partial Fraction Decomposition

    Example 41. PFD Example.
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{s(s+1)}=\frac{1}{s}-\frac{1}{s+1} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}^{-1}=1-e^{-t} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection Unit Step Functions

    \begin{equation*} u_c(t)=\begin{cases}0,&t<c\\1,&t\ge c\end{cases} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}\{u_c(t)f(t-c)\}=e^{-cs}F(s) \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Subsubsection Convolution

    \begin{equation*} (f*g)(t)=\int_0^t f(\tau)g(t-\tau)d\tau \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}\{f*g\}=F(s)G(s) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Numerical Methods

    Definition 44. Euler’s Method.

    \begin{equation*} y_{n+1}=y_n+h f(t_n,y_n) \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Definition 45. Error Measures.

    \begin{equation*} \text{Absolute Error}=|y-y_h| \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{Relative Error}=\frac{|y-y_h|}{|y|} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 42. Euler Method.

    \begin{equation*} y'=y,\quad y(0)=1,\quad h=0.1 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} y_1=1+0.1(1)=1.1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    f = @(t,y) y;
h = 0.1;
tE = 0:h:1;
yE = zeros(size(tE));
yE(1)=1;
for n=1:length(tE)-1
    yE(n+1)=yE(n)+h*yE(n);
end
[tO,yO]=ode45(f,[0 1],1);
plot(tE,yE,'ro-')
hold on
plot(tO,yO,'b')
plot(tE,exp(tE),'k')
legend('Euler','ode45','Exact')

    Activity 6. Investigating Error and Step Size.

    1. Apply Euler’s method with several step sizes.
      🔗
    2. Estimate the solution at \(t=1\text{.}\)
      🔗
    3. Compare with the exact solution.
      🔗
    4. Discuss how the error changes as \(h\) decreases.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Systems of differential equations may be solved using eigenvalues and eigenvectors. Laplace transforms provide an alternative approach for solving initial value problems, while numerical methods provide approximations when exact solutions are difficult or unavailable.
