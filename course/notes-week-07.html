    Section Week 7: Second-order linear ODEs

    Subsection Introduction to Second-Order Differential Equations

    After studying this section, you should be able to:
    🔗
    1. Define a second-order differential equation.
      🔗
    2. Distinguish between linear and nonlinear differential equations.
      🔗
    3. Distinguish between homogeneous and nonhomogeneous equations.
      🔗
    4. Explain why exponential functions are used to solve constant-coefficient differential equations.
      🔗
    5. Form the characteristic equation.
      🔗
    6. Determine solutions corresponding to distinct real roots, repeated roots, and complex roots.
      🔗
    7. Describe common methods used to solve nonhomogeneous equations.
      🔗
    8. Apply variation of parameters to find particular solutions.
      🔗
    A second-order differential equation is an equation involving the second derivative of an unknown function. Many mathematical models from physics and engineering naturally lead to second-order differential equations.
    🔗

    Definition 125. Second-Order Differential Equation.

    A second-order differential equation is an equation involving the second derivative of an unknown function.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} F(x,y,y',y'')=0 \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 126. Examples.

    The following are second-order differential equations.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y''+4y'-5y=0 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} x^2y''+xy'-3y=\sin x \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} y''=(y')^2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Linear Second-Order Differential Equations

    The standard form of a linear second-order differential equation is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a(x)y''+b(x)y'+c(x)y=g(x) \end{equation*}
    where \(a(x)\neq 0\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 127. Homogeneous Equation.

    If \(g(x)=0\text{,}\) then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a(x)y''+b(x)y'+c(x)y=0 \end{equation*}
    is called a homogeneous differential equation.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 128. Nonhomogeneous Equation.

    If \(g(x)\neq 0\text{,}\) then the equation is called nonhomogeneous.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 129. A Nonhomogeneous Equation.

    \begin{equation*} y''-3y'+2y=e^x \end{equation*}
    This is a nonhomogeneous linear differential equation.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Constant-Coefficient Equations

    In this part we focus primarily on equations of the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} ay''+by'+cy=0 \end{equation*}
    where \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) and \(c\) are constants.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Why Exponential Functions?

    Exponential functions are useful because differentiation preserves their basic form.
    🔗
    Suppose
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y=e^{\lambdax} \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y'=\lambda e^{\lambda x} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} y''=\lambda^2 e^{\lambda x} \end{equation*}
    Substituting these expressions into
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} ay''+by'+cy=0 \end{equation*}
    gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a(\lambda^2e^{\lambda x})+b(\lambda e^{\lambda x})+ce^{\lambda x}=0 \end{equation*}
    Factoring out \(e^{\lambda x}\) yields
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} e^{\lambda x}(a\lambda^2+b\lambda+c)=0 \end{equation*}
    Since \(e^{\lambda x}\neq0\text{,}\) we obtain
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a\lambda^2+b\lambda+c=0 \end{equation*}
    This algebraic equation is called the characteristic equation.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Solutions from Characteristic Roots

    Theorem 130. Characteristic Root Cases.

    Consider
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} ay''+by'+cy=0. \end{equation*}
    Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a\lambda^2+b\lambda+c=0 \end{equation*}
    be its characteristic equation.
    🔗
    1. If the roots are distinct real numbers \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\text{,}\) then
      \begin{equation*} y=C_1e^{\lambda_1x}+C_2e^{\lambda_2x}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    2. If the root \(\lambda\) is repeated, then
      \begin{equation*} y=C_1e^{\lambda x}+C_2xe^{\lambda x}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    3. If the roots are \(\alpha\pm\beta i\text{,}\) then
      \begin{equation*} y=e^{\alpha x} \left( C_1\cos(\beta x) + C_2\sin(\beta x) \right). \end{equation*}
      🔗
    🔗
    To determine the constants \(C_1 \) and \(C_2 \text{,}\) we need two conditions, usually given as
    \begin{equation*} y(x_0)=y_0\quad\text{and}\quad y'(x_0)=y_1. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 131.

    \begin{equation*} y''-3y'+2y=0,\quad y(0)=1,\quad y'(0)=0. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    The characteristic equation is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lambda^2-3\lambda+2=0. \end{equation*}
    The roots are \(\lambda_1=1\) and \(\lambda_2=2\text{.}\) Thus the general solution is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y=C_1e^x+C_2e^{2x}. \end{equation*}
    Using the initial conditions, we have
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y(0)=C_1+C_2=1 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} y'(0)=C_1+2C_2=0 \end{equation*}
    Solving this system of equations, we find \(C_1=2\) and \(C_2=-1\text{.}\) Therefore, the solution is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y=2e^x-e^{2x}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Methods for Nonhomogeneous Equations

    For a nonhomogeneous equation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} ay''+by'+cy=g(x), \end{equation*}
    the general solution has the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y=y_c+y_p, \end{equation*}
    where \(y_c\) is the complementary solution and \(y_p\) is a particular solution.
    🔗
    Common methods for finding particular solutions include:
    🔗
    • Method of Undetermined Coefficients
      🔗
    • Variation of Parameters
      🔗
    • Reduction of Order
      🔗
    • Power Series Methods
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Variation of Parameters

    Variation of parameters is one of the most general methods for finding particular solutions of linear differential equations. Unlike undetermined coefficients, it may be used for variable- coefficient equations.
    🔗
    Consider
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y''+p(x)y'+q(x)y=g(x). \end{equation*}
    Suppose the corresponding homogeneous equation has linearly independent solutions \(y_1(x)\) and \(y_2(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y_c=C_1y_1+C_2y_2. \end{equation*}
    Variation of parameters replaces the constants by functions:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y_p=u_1(x)y_1(x)+u_2(x)y_2(x). \end{equation*}
    Define the Wronskian
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} W=\begin{vmatrix} y_1 & y_2 \\ y_1' & y_2' \end{vmatrix}=y_1y_2'-y_1'y_2. \end{equation*}
    The functions \(u_1\) and \(u_2\) satisfy
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u_1'=-\frac{y_2g}{W} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} u_2'=\frac{y_1g}{W} \end{equation*}
    Integrating gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u_1=\int -\frac{y_2g}{W}\,dx \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} u_2=\int \frac{y_1g}{W}\,dx \end{equation*}
    A particular solution is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y_p=u_1y_1+u_2y_2, \end{equation*}
    and the complete solution is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y=y_c+y_p. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 132. Variation of Parameters for a Variable-Coefficient Equation.

    Solve
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2y''-2xy'+2y=x^2, \qquad x>0. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    Divide by \(x^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y''-\frac{2}{x}y'+\frac{2}{x^2}y=1. \end{equation*}
    Solve the homogeneous equation by trying \(y=x^{\lambda}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lambda(\lambda-1)-2\lambda+2=0 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \lambda^2-3\lambda+2=0 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} (\lambda-1)(\lambda-2)=0. \end{equation*}
    Thus
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y_1=x, \qquad y_2=x^2. \end{equation*}
    The complementary solution is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y_c=C_1x+C_2x^2. \end{equation*}
    Compute the Wronskian.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} W= \begin{vmatrix} x & x^2\\ 1 & 2x \end{vmatrix} =x^2. \end{equation*}
    Since \(g(x)=1\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u_1' = -\frac{x^2}{x^2} = -1 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} u_2' = \frac{x}{x^2} = \frac1x \end{equation*}
    Integrating gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u_1=-x, \qquad u_2=\ln x. \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y_p = (-x)(x)+(\ln x)(x^2) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} y_p=-x^2+x^2\ln x. \end{equation*}
    Since \(-x^2\) belongs to the complementary solution, we may absorb it into the constant \(C_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hence,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y_p=x^2\ln x. \end{equation*}
    The solution is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y=C_1x+C_2x^2+x^2\ln x. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
