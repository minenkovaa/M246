    Section Week 2: Determinants; inverses; rank

    In this module, we will discuss how to:
    🔗
    • use Gaussian Elimination to determine whether a matrix is invertible,
      🔗
    • find the inverse of an invertible matrix,
      🔗
    • compute the determinant of a given matrix,
      🔗
    • use the determinant to determine whether a matrix is invertible,
      🔗
    • analyze the determinant of a product and inverses of matrices.
      🔗

    Subsection Invertibility of a Matrix

    A square matrix \(A\) (size \(n\times n\)) is invertible or nonsingular if there exists an \(n\times n\) matrix \(C\) such that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} CA = I_n \quad\text{and}\quad AC = I_n. \end{equation*}
    That matrix is unique and is denoted \(A^{-1}\text{.}\) If no such matrix exists, then \(A\) is singular.
    🔗

    Example 22. The 2 × 2 Case.

    If
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A= \begin{bmatrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    and \(ad-bc\neq 0\text{,}\) then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad-bc} \begin{bmatrix} d & -b\\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 23. Finding an Inverse.

    Find the inverse of
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} B= \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1\\ 4 & 2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    Since
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \det(B)=3\cdot2-1\cdot4=2\neq0, \end{equation*}
    the matrix is invertible and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} B^{-1} = \frac12 \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1\\ -4 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -\frac12\\ -2 & \frac32 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    A quick verification gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} BB^{-1}=I_2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Properties of Inverse Matrices

    Subsubsection The Inverse of an Inverse

    Theorem 24.
    If \(A\) is invertible, then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (A^{-1})^{-1}=A. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Proof.
    Since
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A^{-1}A=I \quad\text{and}\quad AA^{-1}=I, \end{equation*}
    the matrix \(A\) acts as the inverse of \(A^{-1}\text{.}\) Hence
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (A^{-1})^{-1}=A. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection Inverse of a Product

    Theorem 25.
    \begin{equation*} (AB)^{-1}=B^{-1}A^{-1}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Proof.
    \begin{equation*} (AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I. \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} (B^{-1}A^{-1})(AB) = B^{-1}(A^{-1}A)B = B^{-1}B = I. \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (AB)^{-1}=B^{-1}A^{-1}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Elementary Row Operations and Row Equivalence

    Three elementary row operations:
    🔗
    • Add a multiple of one row to another row.
      🔗
    • Interchange two rows.
      🔗
    • Multiply a row by a nonzero constant.
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Row-Echelon and Reduced Row-Echelon Forms

    Definition 26.

    A matrix is in REF if:
    🔗
    • All nonzero rows are above zero rows.
      🔗
    • Leading entries move to the right as rows descend.
      🔗
    • Entries below a leading entry are zero.
      🔗
    A matrix is in RREF if additionally:
    🔗
    • Every leading entry equals 1.
      🔗
    • Each leading 1 is the only nonzero entry in its column.
      🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 27.

    Every matrix is row-equivalent to exactly one RREF.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Computing \(A^{-1}\) via Augmented Matrices

    Method. Construct \(\bigl[A \mid I\bigr]\) and row-reduce:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[A \mid I\right] \xrightarrow{\text{row reduce}} \left[I \mid A^{-1}\right]. \end{equation*}
    If you cannot reach \(I\) on the left, then \(A\) is not invertible.
    🔗
    Note. For \(2\times 2\) matrices, the closed-form formula is often faster, but the augmented matrix method works for square matrices of any size.
    🔗

    Example 28. Example: Finding the Inverse of a 3 × 3 Matrix.

    We now compute the inverse of a \(3\times 3\) matrix using the augmented matrix method.
    🔗
    Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 3\\ 1 & 0 & 2\\ 3 & 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    To find \(A^{-1}\) (if it exists), we row-reduce the augmented matrix \(\bigl[A\mid I_3\bigr]\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 2 & 1 & 3 & 1 & 0 & 0\\ 1 & 0 & 2 & 0 & 1 & 0\\ 3 & 1 & 4 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right]. \end{equation*}
    Step 1: Make a pivot in row 1 (swap \(R_1\) and \(R_2\)).
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 0 & 2 & 0 & 1 & 0\\ 2 & 1 & 3 & 1 & 0 & 0\\ 3 & 1 & 4 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right]. \end{equation*}
    Step 2: Zero out below pivot 1 using \(R_2\gets R_2-2R_1\) and \(R_3\gets R_3-3R_1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 0 & 2 & 0 & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & -1 & 1 & -2 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & -2 & 0 & -3 & 1 \end{array} \right]. \end{equation*}
    Step 3: Fix the pivot in row 2 and eliminate below it: \(R_3\gets R_3-R_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 0 & 2 & 0 & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & -1 & 1 & -2 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & -1 & -1 & -1 & 1 \end{array} \right]. \end{equation*}
    Step 4: Make the pivot in row 3 equal to 1 by \(R_3\gets -R_3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 0 & 2 & 0 & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & -1 & 1 & -2 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 & 1 & -1 \end{array} \right]. \end{equation*}
    Step 5: Clear above the pivot in column 3.
    🔗
    Use \(R_1\gets R_1-2R_3\) and \(R_2\gets R_2+R_3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 0 & 0 & -2 & -1 & 2\\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 2 & -1 & -1\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 & 1 & -1 \end{array} \right]. \end{equation*}
    The left block is now \(I_3\text{,}\) so the right block is \(A^{-1}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & -1 & 2\\ 2 & -1 & -1\\ 1 & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Check your work. A quick check confirms
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} AA^{-1}=I_3. \end{equation*}
    Thus \(A\) is invertible, and the computation is correct.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Determinants

    Concept. The determinant is a single number attached to a square matrix that (among many roles) measures how the associated linear map scales area (in \(\mathbb{R}^2\)) or volume (in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)). If \(\det(A)=0\text{,}\) then \(A\) flattens some dimension and is not invertible.
    🔗

    Subsubsection How to Compute Determinants

    For \(2\times2\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \det \begin{bmatrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{bmatrix} = ad-bc. \end{equation*}
    Cofactor expansion (Laplace). For \(A=[a_{ij}]_{n\times n}\) and \(n\ge 2\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \det(A) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} (-1)^{1+j} a_{1j}\det(A_{1j}), \end{equation*}
    where \(A_{1j}\) is the matrix obtained by deleting row \(1\) and column \(j\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Cofactor expansion diagram (first row).
    🔗
    Example 29. Cofactor Expansion Example.
    \begin{equation*} \det \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 0\\ 5 & 0 & 2\\ 2 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = 1\cdot \det \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2\\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} - 3\cdot \det \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2\\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + 0\cdot(\cdots) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = 1(0\cdot1-2\cdot1) - 3(5\cdot1-2\cdot2) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = -2-3(1) = -5. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Theorem 30. Cofactor Expansion Along Any Row or Column.
    Row expansion: For any fixed row \(i\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \det(A) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij}C_{ij}. \end{equation*}
    Column expansion: For any fixed column \(j\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \det(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{ij}C_{ij}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Example 31. Determinant of a 4 × 4 Matrix.
    Find the determinant of
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A= \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 7 & 1 & 1\\ 0 & 2 & 2 & 2\\ 0 & 0 & 3 & 2\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Using cofactor expansion along the first row:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \det(A) = 3\det(A_{11}) - 7\det(A_{12}) + \det(A_{13}) - \det(A_{14}). \end{equation*}
    Compute each cofactor.
    🔗
    The submatrix \(A_{11}\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 & 2\\ 0 & 3 & 2\\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad C_{11}=(+1)\det(A_{11}). \end{equation*}
    The submatrix \(A_{12}\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & 2\\ 0 & 3 & 2\\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad C_{12}=(-1)\det(A_{12}). \end{equation*}
    The submatrix \(A_{13}\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & 2\\ 0 & 0 & 2\\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad C_{13}=(+1)\det(A_{13}). \end{equation*}
    The submatrix \(A_{14}\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & 2\\ 0 & 0 & 3\\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad C_{14}=(-1)\det(A_{14}). \end{equation*}
    Now compute the determinants of the \(3\times3\) matrices:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \det(A_{11}) = 2 \det \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2\\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} - 2 \det \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + 2 \det \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 3\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = 2(3\cdot1-2\cdot1). \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \det(A_{11})=2. \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \det(A_{12}) = 0\cdot \det \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2\\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} - 2\cdot \det \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + 2\cdot \det \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 3\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = 0. \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \det(A_{13}) = 0\cdot \det \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} - 2\cdot \det \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + 2\cdot \det \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = 0. \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \det(A_{14}) = 0\cdot \det \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 3\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} - 2\cdot \det \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 3\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + 2\cdot \det \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = 0. \end{equation*}
    Thus the full cofactor expansion gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \det(A) = 3(2)-7(0)+1(0)-1(0) = 6. \end{equation*}
    As an alternative, let us use the first column expansion:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \det(A) = \det \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 7 & 1 & 1\\ 0 & 2 & 2 & 2\\ 0 & 0 & 3 & 2\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = 3\det \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 & 2\\ 0 & 3 & 2\\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} +0+0+0 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = 3\cdot2 \det \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2\\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = 3\cdot2\cdot1 = 6. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Row-operations and determinants.
    🔗
    • Add a multiple of one row to another: the determinant is unchanged.
      🔗
    • Swap two rows: the determinant changes sign.
      🔗
    • Multiply a row by \(k\text{:}\) the determinant is multiplied by \(k\text{.}\)
      🔗
    Row-operation diagrams (applied to a 2 × 2 matrix).
    🔗
    Add a multiple of one row: \(\mathrm{Row}_2\to\mathrm{Row}_2+k\mathrm{Row}_1\) keeps the determinant unchanged.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{vmatrix} 1&2\\3& 4\end{vmatrix}=\begin{vmatrix}1+5\cdot3&2+5\cdot4\\3&4\end{vmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Swap two rows: the sign of the determinant flips.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{vmatrix} 1&2\\3& 4\end{vmatrix}=-\begin{vmatrix}3& 4\\1&2\end{vmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Scale one row: the determinant scales by the same factor.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{vmatrix} 5&10\\3& 4\end{vmatrix}=5\cdot \begin{vmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{vmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Determinant and Invertibility and Product/Inverse Rules

    Invertibility test. A square matrix \(A\) is invertible if and only if \(\det(A)\neq 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Useful properties.
    🔗
    • \(\det(A^\top)=\det(A)\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • If \(A\) and \(B\) are \(n\times n\) matrices, then \(\det(AB)=\det(A)\det(B)\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • If \(A\) is invertible, then \(\det(A^{-1})=\dfrac{1}{\det(A)}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • If \(A\) is triangular, then \(\det(A)\) is the product of its diagonal entries.
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Characterization of Invertible Matrices (Equivalences)

    For an \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\text{,}\) the following twelve statements are all equivalent. This collection is sometimes called the Inverse Matrix Theorem. Any one of these being true guarantees all the others.
    🔗
    1. \(A\) is invertible.
      🔗
    2. \(A\) is row equivalent to the identity matrix \(I_n\text{.}\)
      🔗
    3. \(A\) has \(n\) pivot positions (one in each row and each column).
      🔗
    4. The equation \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\) has only the trivial solution \(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    5. The columns of \(A\) are linearly independent.
      🔗
    6. The linear transformation \(T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}\) is one-to-one.
      🔗
    7. The equation \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\) has at least one solution for every \(\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^n\text{.}\)
      🔗
    8. The columns of \(A\) span \(\mathbb{R}^n\text{.}\)
      🔗
    9. The linear transformation \(T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}\) maps \(\mathbb{R}^n\) onto \(\mathbb{R}^n\text{.}\)
      🔗
    10. There exists a matrix \(C\) such that \(CA=I_n\) (that is, \(A\) has a left inverse).
      🔗
    11. There exists a matrix \(D\) such that \(AD=I_n\) (that is, \(A\) has a right inverse).
      🔗
    12. \(A^\top\) is invertible.
      🔗
    Interpretation. These twelve statements connect algebra, geometry, and linear transformations:
    🔗
    • (1)–(3) are row-operation and pivot statements.
      🔗
    • (4)–(6) relate to one-to-one behavior.
      🔗
    • (7)–(9) relate to onto behavior and existence of solutions.
      🔗
    • (10)–(12) describe left and right inverses and invertibility of the transpose.
      🔗
    Together, they provide many equivalent ways to detect invertibility.
    🔗
