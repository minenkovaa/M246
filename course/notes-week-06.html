    Section Week 6: First-order ODEs (separable, linear, exact)

    Subsection First-Order Ordinary Differential Equations

    Source: These notes are adapted from Remark 115.
    🔗

    Remark 115.

    Paul Dawkins, “First Order Differential Equations”, Paul’s Online Math Notes, https://tutorial.math.lamar.edu/classes/de/introfirstorder.aspx.
    🔗
    🔗
    Differential equations arise when a quantity and its rate of change are related. Examples include population growth, radioactive decay, cooling processes, mixing problems, and engineering systems.
    🔗
    A first-order ordinary differential equation is an equation involving an unknown function \(y(t)\) and its first derivative.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dt}=f(y,t) \end{equation*}
    Unlike algebraic equations, there is no single method that solves all first-order differential equations. Instead, we identify the type of equation and apply an appropriate solution technique.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Modeling with First-Order Differential Equations

    Many engineering and scientific applications lead naturally to first-order differential equations.
    🔗
    • Population growth and decay
      🔗
    • Mixing problems
      🔗
    • Radioactive decay
      🔗
    • Cooling and heating processes
      🔗
    • Velocity of falling objects
      🔗
    The modeling process usually follows these steps:
    🔗
    1. Define the unknown quantity.
      🔗
    2. Identify how the quantity changes over time.
      🔗
    3. Write a differential equation describing the relationship.
      🔗
    4. Solve the differential equation.
      🔗
    5. Interpret the solution in context.
      🔗

    Example 116. Concrete Curing on a Bridge Deck.

    After a bridge deck is poured, engineers monitor the concrete temperature to ensure proper curing. Suppose the temperature \(T(t)\) of the concrete decreases at a rate proportional to the difference between the concrete temperature and the surrounding air temperature of \(20^\circ\text{C}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The rate of cooling is proportional to the temperature difference between the concrete and the surrounding air.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dT}{dt}=-k(T-20), \end{equation*}
    where \(k>0\) is a proportionality constant.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Classifying First-Order Differential Equations

    When presented with a differential equation, the first step is to identify its form.
    🔗
    Some important classes of first-order differential equations include:
    🔗
    • Separable equations
      🔗
    • Linear equations
      🔗
    • Exact equations
      🔗
    • Bernoulli equations
      🔗
    The goal is to recognize which solution method can be applied.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Separable Differential Equations

    Definition 117.

    A differential equation is separable if it can be written in the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} N(y)\frac{dy}{dx}=M(x) \end{equation*}
    or equivalently
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx}=f(x)g(y). \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Subsubsection Solution Strategy

    1. Move all \(y\)-terms to one side and all \(x\)-terms to the other.
      🔗
    2. Integrate both sides.
      🔗
    3. Solve for \(y\text{,}\) if possible.
      🔗
    4. Use the initial condition if one is provided.
      🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 118.

    Classify and solve the differential equation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{x^2+1}{y(y^2+4)}. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    This equation is separable.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y(y^2+4)\,dy=(x^2+1)\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Integrate both sides:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int (y^3+4y)\,dy=\int (x^2+1)\,dx. \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{y^4}{4}+2y^2=\frac{x^3}{3}+x+C. \end{equation*}
    This implicit equation is the general solution.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Linear Differential Equations

    Definition 119.

    A first-order linear differential equation has the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y'+p(x)y=g(x). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    These equations are solved using an integrating factor.
    🔗

    Subsubsection Integrating Factor

    For
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y'+p(x)y=g(x), \end{equation*}
    the integrating factor is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mu(x)=e^{\int p(x)\,dx}. \end{equation*}
    Multiplying the differential equation by \(\mu(x)\) transforms the left-hand side into a product derivative:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}\big(\mu(x)y\big)=\mu(x)g(x). \end{equation*}
    Integrate both sides and solve for \(y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 120.

    Classify and solve the differential equation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (x^2+1)\frac{dy}{dx}+2xy=\sin x. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    Divide by \(x^2+1\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y'+\frac{2x}{x^2+1}y=\frac{\sin x}{x^2+1}. \end{equation*}
    This is a linear first-order differential equation.
    🔗
    The integrating factor is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mu(x) =e^{\int \frac{2x}{x^2+1}\,dx} =e^{\ln(x^2+1)} =x^2+1. \end{equation*}
    Multiplying through by the integrating factor gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}\big((x^2+1)y\big)=\sin x. \end{equation*}
    Integrating:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (x^2+1)y=-\cos x+C. \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} y=\frac{-\cos x+C}{x^2+1}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Exact Differential Equations

    Definition 121.

    A differential equation written as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} M(x,y)\,dx+N(x,y)\,dy=0 \end{equation*}
    is exact if
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    When an equation is exact, there exists a potential function \(F(x,y)\) such that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} dF=M\,dx+N\,dy. \end{equation*}
    The solution is obtained by finding \(F(x,y)\) and setting
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} F(x,y)=C. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Subsection Bernoulli Differential Equations

    Definition 122.

    A Bernoulli equation has the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y'+p(x)y=q(x)y^n, \end{equation*}
    where \(n\neq 0\) and \(n\neq 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    These equations can be transformed into linear equations through the substitution
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} v=y^{1-n}. \end{equation*}
    After substitution, solve the resulting linear differential equation.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 123.

    Classify and solve the differential equation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y'+\frac{2}{x}y=x^3y^{-2}. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    This is a Bernoulli equation with \(n=-2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} v=y^{1-(-2)}=y^3. \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} v'=3y^2y'. \end{equation*}
    Multiply the differential equation by \(3y^2\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} v'+\frac{6}{x}v=3x^3. \end{equation*}
    This is linear in \(v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The integrating factor is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mu(x)=e^{\int 6/x\,dx}=x^6. \end{equation*}
    Multiply through by \(x^6\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}(x^6v)=3x^9. \end{equation*}
    Integrate:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^6v=\frac{3}{10}x^{10}+C. \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} v=\frac{3}{10}x^4+\frac{C}{x^6}. \end{equation*}
    Substitute back \(v=y^3\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y^3=\frac{3}{10}x^4+\frac{C}{x^6}. \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} y=\left(\frac{3}{10}x^4+\frac{C}{x^6}\right)^{1/3}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Initial Value Problems

    Definition 124.

    An initial value problem (IVP) consists of a differential equation together with an initial condition.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx}=f(x,y), \qquad y(x_0)=y_0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The differential equation determines a family of solutions, while the initial condition identifies one specific solution.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Numerical Approximation: Euler’s Method

    Not all differential equations can be solved analytically. One common numerical technique is Euler’s Method.
    🔗
    Starting from \((x_n,y_n)\text{,}\) the next approximation is computed using
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y_{n+1} = y_n+h\,f(x_n,y_n), \end{equation*}
    where \(h\) is the step size.
    🔗
    Euler’s Method follows the tangent line to generate approximate solution values.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Summary

    When solving a first-order differential equation:
    🔗
    1. Identify the type of differential equation.
      🔗
    2. Select an appropriate solution method.
      🔗
    3. Find the general solution.
      🔗
    4. Apply initial conditions when given.
      🔗
    5. Interpret the solution in context.
      🔗
    The most common first-order differential equations encountered in an introductory course are separable, linear, exact, and Bernoulli equations. Recognizing the structure of an equation is often the most important step in finding its solution.
