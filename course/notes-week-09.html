    Subsection Introduction to Partial Differential Equations

    Definition 46. Partial Differential Equation.

    A partial differential equation (PDE) is an equation involving an unknown function of several independent variables and one or more partial derivatives.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} F(x,y,u,u_x,u_y,u_{xx},u_{xy},\ldots)=0 \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 43. ODE versus PDE.

    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dt}=y \end{equation*}
    This is an ODE because there is only one independent variable.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u_t=u_{xx} \end{equation*}
    This is a PDE because the solution depends on both position and time.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Order and Linearity

    Definition 47. Order.

    The order of a PDE is determined by its highest derivative.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Classification of a Second-Order PDE in Three Variables

    Consider the second-order partial differential equation
    \begin{equation*} u_{xy}+u_{xz}-u_{yz}=0. \end{equation*}
    To classify the equation, we examine only its principal (second-order) part and write it in the standard form
    \begin{equation*} Au_{xx}+Bu_{yy}+Cu_{zz} +2Du_{xy}+2Eu_{xz}+2Fu_{yz}=0. \end{equation*}
    Comparing coefficients gives
    \begin{equation*} A=B=C=0,\qquad D=\frac12,\qquad E=\frac12,\qquad F=-\frac12. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The associated symmetric coefficient matrix is
    \begin{equation*} M= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \frac12 & \frac12\\ \frac12 & 0 & -\frac12\\ \frac12 & -\frac12 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The eigenvalues of this matrix are
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_1=\frac12,\qquad \lambda_2=\frac12,\qquad \lambda_3=-1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Following the multidimensional classification criterion, one determines the type of the PDE from the signs of the eigenvalues of the coefficient matrix. As noted by Walet, in dimensions higher than two, elliptic equations have eigenvalues of the same sign, hyperbolic equations have eigenvalues with mixed signs, and parabolic equations have at least one zero eigenvalue. Since the matrix \(M\) has two positive eigenvalues and one negative eigenvalue, its signature is
    \begin{equation*} (+,+,-). \end{equation*}
    Therefore the equation is hyperbolic.
    🔗
    The partial differential equation
    \begin{equation*} u_{xy}+u_{xz}-u_{yz}=0 \end{equation*}
    is hyperbolic.
    🔗

    Remark 152.

    \(B^2-AC\)
    🔗
    Following the classification criterion for PDEs in more than two variables, one examines the eigenvalues of the symmetric coefficient matrix associated with the principal part of the equation. If all eigenvalues have the same sign, the equation is elliptic; if the signs are mixed, it is hyperbolic; and if at least one eigenvalue is zero, it is parabolic. See Walet, "2.3: More than 2D".

    Subsection Separation of Variables

    Definition 48. Separated Solution.

    \begin{equation*} u(x,t)=X(x)T(t) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Substituting a product solution into a PDE often reduces the problem to two ordinary differential equations.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Heat Equation Example

    Example 44. Heat Equation on a Finite Rod.

    Solve
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u_t=u_{xx}, \qquad 0<x<L, \end{equation*}
    with boundary conditions
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u(0,t)=0, \qquad u(L,t)=0, \end{equation*}
    and initial condition
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u(x,0)=f(x). \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    Assume a separated solution
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u(x,t)=X(x)T(t). \end{equation*}
    Substituting gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} XT'=X''T. \end{equation*}
    Dividing by \(XT\) yields
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{T'}{T}=\frac{X''}{X}=-\lambda. \end{equation*}
    Two ordinary differential equations follow:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} X''+\lambda X=0, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} T'+\lambda T=0. \end{equation*}
    The boundary conditions imply
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} X_n(x)=\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right). \end{equation*}
    The time-dependent factors are
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T_n(t)=e^{-(n\pi/L)^2t}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) e^{-(n\pi/L)^2t}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 157. Interpretation.

    Each Fourier mode decays exponentially. Higher-frequency modes decay more rapidly, causing the temperature profile to become smoother over time.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection MATLAB Visualization of Heat Diffusion 

    L = 1;
x = linspace(0,L,100);
t = linspace(0,0.5,100);
[X,T] = meshgrid(x,t);
U = exp(-pi^2*T).*sin(pi*X);
surf(X,T,U)
xlabel('Position')
ylabel('Time')
zlabel('Temperature')
title('Heat Equation Solution')
shading interp
colorbar
    🔗
    Partial differential equations model quantities that vary in both space and time. Second-order PDEs are classified as elliptic, parabolic, or hyperbolic. The heat equation provides a fundamental example of a parabolic PDE and can be solved using separation of variables and Fourier series.
